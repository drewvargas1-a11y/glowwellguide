TENS (Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation) delivers mild electrical pulses through the skin that interrupt pain signals traveling to the brain — providing drug-free relief for back pain, neck pain, arthritis, sciatica, and post-workout muscle soreness. Physical therapists use TENS clinically, and at-home devices now deliver professional-grade stimulation at consumer prices.

We tested four devices on back pain, shoulder tension, and post-exercise muscle recovery across multiple users, evaluating intensity range, mode variety, pad adhesion quality, battery life, and overall ease of use for first-time users.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most TENS sessions should last 15–30 minutes for acute pain and up to 60 minutes for chronic pain. Don't exceed 60 continuous minutes on the same area — nerve fibers adapt and the pain-blocking effect diminishes (called accommodation). For muscle recovery (EMS mode), 15–20 minutes per muscle group is standard. Multiple sessions per day (2–4) are safe for chronic conditions.
Never use TENS on the front of the neck (carotid sinus), across the chest, over the spine or skull, on broken skin, over metal implants or pacemakers, during pregnancy (especially abdomen/lower back), or while driving. Consult a doctor before starting TENS if you have epilepsy, cancer, heart disease, or are pregnant.
TENS (Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation) stimulates sensory nerves to block pain signals — it doesn't contract muscles visibly. EMS (Electrical Muscle Stimulation) directly contracts muscles for recovery, strength training, or rehabilitation. Many modern devices combine both. For pain management, use TENS mode. For muscle recovery and athletic performance, use EMS mode.
